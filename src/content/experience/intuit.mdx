## Mandates don't work here

Nobody disagreed that duplicated tooling was worth fixing. It just never rose above whatever
was already on someone's plate — and requiring it wouldn't have changed that, it would only
have made people resent the thing I wanted them to care about.

## So I made it a tournament

It ran over a couple of months, with each team completing a task every week against a
**published criteria model** — so "ready" was a definition anyone could check themselves
against, rather than an opinion someone else held.

I tallied points and kept a running leaderboard. At the end, teams presented to a panel of
judges, and the winning team took internal budget, swag, and — the part that actually moved
people — praise routed to their managers.

## The real design problem was the two hours

Every participant was giving up **a couple of hours a week on top of their existing
workload.** That's the whole constraint. None of this works unless what you're asking for is
visibly worth more than the time it costs, so the leaderboard, the weekly cadence and the
judged finale weren't decoration — they were what made the cost legible and made progress
feel like it was going somewhere.

Sourcing workshop hosts was harder than expected, because the org spans India, California and
the East Coast. Some workshops I ran twice; others I recorded. A program that only works in
one timezone isn't a program, it's a meeting.

## What it produced

**150 engineers took part, and 40% of capabilities reached the highest tier of the criteria
model** — documented, covered, and ready for another team to pick up without asking anyone's
permission.

## It ran a second time

I was asked to run another tournament shortly before I left, and participation was higher —
teams had heard about the first one and wanted in.

That's the result I'd point at over any percentage. The strongest evidence that an optional
program worked is being asked to run it again by people nobody told to ask.
