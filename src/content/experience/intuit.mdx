## What I learned

The platform team wanted more developers onboarded and higher maturity scores. What I heard
from developers was different: most didn't know the platform existed, they worked only in
their own repo, the tools on it carried a lot of tech debt, and nothing gave them a reason to
go back and meet a new standard. **It felt optional, not urgent.** The model itself was
complex and heavily documented, so the job was making it easy to take in and easy to act on.

## Decisions

All the data already existed. The design work was reframing it. I walk through a reconstruction of the board in interviews.


**Level names, not just numbers.** L4 reads as Self-Serve, meaning another team can pick
the tool up without asking anyone. The board teaches the model without anyone having to look
it up.

**Unchecked criteria stay visible.** Each one links to how to complete it on the developer
portal, so the board tells you what to do next, not just where you rank.

**Movement sits beside the score.** The tournament rewarded levels gained as well as
points, so progress was visible even for teams far from the top.

## Outcome

**150 engineers took part, across 34 capabilities, and 40% reached Level 4 (Self-Serve) or
higher.** Every team in the eight-team pilot moved up at least one level, and I was asked to run
the tournament a second time.
