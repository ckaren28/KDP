## What I learned

I sat down with the travel manager to see how requests were actually processed, and
interviewed several of the employees who traveled most. Four things stood out:

- Trips had several legs, and the old form assumed a simple out-and-back.
- Travelers weren't sure whether to enter times in their own time zone or the destination's.
- Changing a request meant starting a new one from scratch.
- Requests needed a manager's approval, and a way to comment on and resubmit them.

## Decisions

<p class="why">Heard: trips had several legs, and time zones confused people.</p>

**One request, several legs.** The form holds as many segments as the trip needs, single legs
and round trips together. Each leg has labeled departure and arrival times with guidance on
which location's time to use, and an airport-code lookup instead of free text.

<p class="why">Heard: changing a request meant starting over, and every trip needed a manager's approval.</p>

**A request that lives, not a one-off form.** Managers approve or decline from a notification
that carries the trip's essentials, and a request can be commented on, updated and resubmitted
instead of started over.

<p class="why">Heard: the travel manager was absorbing everything over email.</p>

**Two views, two jobs.** Employees got the form and a table of their own requests. The travel
manager got every request in one place, filterable, with approve and decline right there, so
approvals stopped happening over email. Color told you which side you were on: red for
employees, blue for the manager.

## Outcome

I built the front end in Vue, JavaScript, SASS and GraphQL, responsive so it worked on a phone
as well as at a desk. WET's IT team tested it first, it was piloted in a couple of departments,
and then it rolled out company-wide. The old system was retired.

## Also at WET: the intranet
