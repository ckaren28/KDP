## What I was trying to find out

Two pages were losing people: the **requirements** page, where travellers check what a
visa needs, and the **application funnel** itself. Analytics could say they left. It
couldn't say why.

## Round one

I put open-ended polls on both pages and let them run for six weeks. They returned
**420 responses** — 291 on requirements, 129 in the funnel.

The volume was workable but the answers were vague. Opening with an open question asks
someone to compose a thought before they've agreed to participate at all, and most
people simply don't.

## Changing the instrument, not the sample

For round two I inverted the structure: lead with a **binary yes/no**, then ask the open
question as a follow-up to whoever answered.

The effect was immediate. **One week of round two drew more than double what five weeks
of round one had.** Roughly half of the people who answered the first question went on
to answer the second, which meant the qualitative pool grew alongside the quantitative
one instead of competing with it.

The answers also changed character. Round one produced procedural questions — people
asking how to use the service. Round two produced declarative ones from people who had
already found what they needed and hit a specific wall. More useful, and more specific.

<p class="dv-cap">Mentions, coded from open-text responses on the requirements page</p>

- **Requirements not clear** — the largest single category
- **Cost not clear** — people couldn't tell what the total would be, service fees included
- **Processing time not clear**
- **Can't find the documents** — letters of invitation and business cover letters especially
- **Too time-consuming** — the theme both rounds agreed on

Underneath those sat a repeated request the site had no answer for: *let me save this and
come back*. People were gathering passports, photos and employer letters over days, and
the funnel assumed a single sitting.

## What I'd do differently

The round-one design was the mistake, and it cost five weeks to find out. I'd now treat
the first question as a participation decision rather than a research question — earn the
response first, ask for the thinking second.
